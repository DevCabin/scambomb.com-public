Key takeaways

A real Bank of America email comes from a @bankofamerica.com sender address, and every link inside points to a bankofamerica.com URL. If either is off, the greeting is generic, or the message pushes urgent action, you have a fake. Do not click anything; forward it to abuse@bankofamerica.com and delete it.

How can I tell if an email really came from Bank of America?

Run a 60-second routine before touching anything: check the sender's full address, hover over every link to see where it really goes, and match the message to your actual account by opening the bank's app or typing the website yourself. A real alert will still be sitting in your inbox or notifications when you log in directly. If it isn't, the email was fake no matter how convincing it looked.

The three checks that catch almost every fake

First, the sender domain. Real Bank of America mail comes from addresses ending in @bankofamerica.com, and scammers know people glance rather than read, so they use lookalikes like bankofamerica-secure.com or rnbankofamerica.com with a swapped letter. Second, the links. On a computer, hover your mouse over the button or "verify your account" link and read the URL preview in the corner of your browser; the true destination, not the displayed text, is what counts.

Anything routing you to a login page that isn't bankofamerica.com is a red flag. Third, the message itself. Urgency is the tell: "act within 24 hours," "your account will be closed," "confirm now." Banks put problems in writing and give you time; they don't punish hesitation.

Why the logo and layout prove nothing

Scammers copy the real logo, colors, and footer text straight from a genuine email, so a professional look means nothing. What they can't fake is your actual account: log in through the app you already use and check whether the alert exists there. What do the sender addresses and link URLs in real BofA phishing emails look like?

Display name vs actual address: the mismatch that matters

Phishing emails almost always show "Bank of America" in the display name, because the display name is just typed text anyone can set. Expand the email (tap the sender name, then "details" in Gmail or Outlook) and look at the actual address behind it. If it reads something like alert@boa-secure-login.com or no-reply@bankofamerica-alerts.net, the display name was a costume.

Reading a link URL before you click it

Hover over any button or link (long-press on a phone) and read the URL from left to right. The domain is the part just before the first single slash, so bankofamerica.com.verify-login.ru is really a .ru site. Compare what you see against the table below.

What you see	What it tells you
bankofamerica.com/...	Genuine domain, though the page itself still deserves a check
bankofamerica-secure.com	Lookalike: hyphenated additions are never BofA
bankofarnerica.com	Character-swap lookalike ("rn" posing as "m")
boa.verify-account.info	Real domain is verify-account.info; "boa" is a decoy

When the sender address looks legitimate

Some fakes arrive from genuine-looking addresses like service@bankofamerica.com, because sender addresses can be forged. If the address looks right but the email asks you to log in through a link or confirm your PIN, treat the request itself as the red flag and type the bank's web address yourself.

Why did I get a 'suspicious activity' or 'account suspended' email if I don't bank with BofA?

Because nobody targeted you personally. Scammers buy or harvest millions of email addresses and send the same fake "suspicious activity" alert to all of them, betting that enough recipients happen to have a Bank of America account to make it worthwhile. Receiving one says nothing about your accounts; it only confirms your address is on a sprayed list. That is also why the email feels generic: no partial account number, no name, no transaction details a real bank would include. If your parent gets one, the right move is to delete it, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or by forwarding to reportphishing@ftc.gov (FTC). Which email details do scammers get wrong most often in bank phishing attempts?

Ranked from most to least reliable, the tells are:

1. Urgency deadlines. Real banks do not give you 24 hours to "verify your account" or your card gets frozen. A countdown clock is the clearest red flag in the message.
2. Generic greetings. "Dear Customer" or "Dear Valued Member" instead of your name. Your bank knows your name and uses it.
3. Mismatched links. Hover over the button (long-press on mobile) and read the actual URL. Anything that is not a Bank of America domain is fake, no matter how official the button looks.
4. Reply-to addresses. The display name says Bank of America; the reply-to is a Gmail or Outlook account. Banks never ask you to reply by email.
5. Sender domain. Look for small misspellings like "bankofamerica-secure.com" or a hyphenated lookalike.

Attachments and the file types banks never send

Banks do not send statements, invoices, or "security notices" as.zip,.exe,.html, or.doc attachments. Any attachment claiming to be from a bank is a scam or malware, full stop. How can I check a suspicious email safely without clicking anything?

Hover, don't click: previewing links on desktop and mobile

On a computer, rest your mouse pointer over the button or link and read the URL that appears in the corner of your screen. A real Bank of America email points to bankofamerica.com; a fake points to lookalike domains or unrelated hosts.

On an iPhone, press and hold a link until a preview pops up, then cancel without releasing. On Android, long-press to copy the link, then paste it into Notes to inspect it. If the preview looks wrong, delete the email.

The manual-URL rule that beats every other check

Never sign in through an email link. Open your browser, type your bank's address yourself, and log in there. If there is a real problem with the account, it will show up in the official app or site. This one habit defeats urgency tactics, spoofed sender names, and cloned pages all at once.

What should I do immediately after clicking a link in a fake Bank of America email?

What you do next depends on one question: did you type anything in?

If you entered your login or card number

Assume the scammers have it. Call Bank of America using the number on your card or the bank's official site, not any number from the email, and have the account locked. Change your password from a different device, then turn on two-factor authentication. If you entered a card number, ask the bank to reissue it. If you entered your Social Security number, place a free fraud alert with one of the three credit bureaus and watch for unfamiliar accounts.

If you clicked but entered nothing

You are probably fine, but run a quick check. Close the tab without interacting with anything on the page. Run a virus scan on your device, since some fake bank pages push downloads. Delete the email so a parent or housemate does not click it later. Watch the account for a week; if anything looks off, treat it as a credentials problem and call the bank.

How do I report a Bank of America phishing email to the right place?

Forward the email to abuse@bankofamerica.com, then delete it. Bank of America asks customers to forward suspicious messages to that address so its security team can confirm whether the email is fake and take action on the sender. If your parent clicked a link or entered credentials, call the number on the back of their card immediately, then file a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov, which the FBI directs people to for reporting internet-related fraud. Each report does something different: the BofA forward helps the bank block the sender, the IC3 complaint builds a federal case record, and marking the message as phishing in Gmail or Outlook helps the provider filter copies to other families. Report in that order if credentials were entered, since the card call stops the damage first.
